# Research design and priority assessment

Cutoff: 14 October 2026. Target: CAG Governing Critical Digital Infrastructures
in the Indo-Pacific workshop, Singapore, 4-5 March 2027.

The call requires 200-300 words plus a short bio by email by 6 November 2026.
Accepted participants later supply an argument-driven 3,000-4,000-word paper.
No specified citation style or paper template; no OpenReview account needed.
All career stages are welcomed. Attendance and discussant obligations are for
both days. The author is described as an independent researcher, without
claiming current institutional appointment from a past undergraduate role.

## Prior-paper review

All PDFs in Downloads/research, including venue variants and dossiers, were
extracted to text and their abstracts reviewed. Topics include cache coupling,
retry reliability, agent delegation, memory, financial exposure, deposit control,
contracts, retrieval fusion, noisy bandits, control compression, replay,
multilingual quotas, healthcare archives, schema fidelity, crawler policies,
and neural feature repair. None of the reviewed abstracts studies Indo-Pacific
licensing, cable repair governance, or electricity wheeling. There is conceptual
adjacency to prior work on dependencies and collective capability, but this paper
does not recycle an agent-market theorem, dataset, or experiment. Its comparative
documentary argument is distinct.

## Literature searches

Queries included submarine cable repair governance/permits/queues in the
Indo-Pacific; Singapore data centres and cross-border electricity; satellite
licensing emergency continuity and renewals; recovery rights; restoration agency;
infrastructure activation gap; permission horizon; temporal sovereignty;
regime complexity; weaponized interdependence; and temporal networks.

Closest work: Noor (2024); CCRC's 2026 cable repair, maintenance and LEO reports;
Farrell and Newman (2019); Alter and Meunier (2009); Holme and Saramaki (2012);
Zhang et al. (2018). The CCRC August 2026 reports already distinguish slow repair
from non-response and discuss regulatory friction, contracts, redundancy and
sovereign capacity. Claiming these observations as discoveries would be wrong.
Existing US renewal procedures also preclude a claim that administrative
continuation is a novel legal invention. The scoped contribution is a comparison
of permission *scope, activation and duration* across Indo-Pacific domains and
an executable distinction between point availability and recovery-period cover.
No exhaustive novelty or patent-priority claim is established.

## Selection and coding

Nine focal public documents, S7-S15, give eight units: ASEAN (two official
summaries), Tonga, Niue, FSM, Sri Lanka, Kiribati, LTMS importer permission and
LTMS wheeling agreement. Selection seeks variation in institutional form and
reported duration, not a representative random sample. Literature and sources
S1-S6 and S16-S18 contextualize the comparison and alternatives. Coding is by
one researcher; there is no intercoder reliability statistic. Unknown values are
explicit. No additive country score, causal estimate or regional prevalence
estimate is computed. Press releases cannot establish private contractual terms.

ASEAN's full 2026 guidelines PDF was identified but direct retrieval returned
403. Provisions are therefore discussed only to the extent supported by S7-S8;
the media-reported ten-day target is excluded from the coded results. Tonga and
Kiribati source PDFs were downloaded and locally extracted; other focal sources
were read with the web tool. Some TNB direct retrievals failed, but its complete
five-page press release was readable through web extraction. The Kiribati
document is explicitly a draft planning disclosure and cannot establish current
2026 commissioning status. Tonga's made date is not its unverified Gazette date.

## Inference and counterarguments

Documents establish legal/administrative design and dated milestones. They do
not establish actual outages caused by expiry. Model inputs are hypothetical
and never fitted to named-country outcomes. Uniform counting of start days is a
coverage measure, not an estimated shock distribution. Temporary permissions
can preserve public accountability; automatic continuation can entrench firms.
Physical capacity, service scope, readiness and provider performance remain
necessary. Conclusions concern conditional assurance, not autarky or guaranteed
resilience. A bridge is a policy proposal with safeguards, not an existing right.
